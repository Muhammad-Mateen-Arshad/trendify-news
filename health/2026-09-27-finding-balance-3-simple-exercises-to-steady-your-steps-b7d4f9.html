Finding balance: 3 simple exercises to steady your steps

Published by AI Pipeline | September 27, 2026

Key Takeaways

Information from Harvard Health Publishing suggests that maintaining physical balance is an essential component of everyday movement for older adults. While many individuals focus on regular walking or strength training, incorporating specific balance routines is equally vital.

  • Balance relies on coordinated actions from your brain, bones, muscles, tendons, eyes, and ears.
  • Age-related physical changes and conditions like inner ear disorders can affect overall stability.
  • Practicing static and dynamic movements two or three times a week can help keep you upright and prevent fall-related injuries.

What the Source Says

According to Harvard Health Publishing, daily tasks such as climbing stairs or reaching for high supermarket shelves require complex physical steadying. As people grow older, the body's internal balance systems become less responsive. Health factors that become more common with age—such as decreased sensation in the feet, inner ear issues, or postural hypotension (low blood pressure upon standing)—can also make individuals feel unsteady.

To support physical stability, experts from Harvard Health Publishing highlight two distinct exercise categories:

  • Static Balance Exercises: These focus on remaining stable while standing still. An example involves placing one foot directly in front of the other in a heel-to-toe position while focusing on a fixed spot straight ahead.
  • Dynamic Balance Exercises: These target balance while the body is in motion. Examples include a sideways braiding movement where you step across your feet, or stepping forward while raising a knee.

Practical Notes

Research suggests that performing these activities two to three times weekly provides beneficial practice for staying steady. Beginners can make movements easier by holding onto the back of a chair or counter for physical support. To increase the challenge over time, individuals can lengthen hold times, close their eyes during static poses, or lift their knees higher during dynamic steps. Talk to your doctor before starting any new exercise routine or if you experience persistent unsteadiness.
