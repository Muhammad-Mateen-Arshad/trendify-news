Creatine may help build muscle even without exercise, study suggests

Published on October 06, 2026

Key Takeaways

  • Research suggests that creatine supplementation may help middle-aged adults build lean muscle mass, even without a regular exercise routine.
  • In a 12-week study, sedentary participants taking creatine gained about 2.4 pounds of lean tissue, while those who also exercised gained about 3 pounds.
  • Creatine use was also associated with gains in strength, cognitive function, and favorable changes in certain blood markers.

What the Source Says

A 12-week trial published in the Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition evaluated 64 healthy, sedentary adults aged 45 to 65. Led by researchers from Texas A&M University, the study examined the impact of taking a creatine supplement twice daily compared to a placebo. Participants followed a calorie-deficit diet guided by American Heart Association principles. They also chose whether to join a supervised training program three times per week.

Research suggests that participants taking creatine without structured exercise gained approximately 2.4 pounds of lean tissue by the end of 12 weeks, along with increased strength on bench press and leg press exercises. Participants who combined creatine with the workout program gained about 3 pounds of lean tissue, reduced their body fat, and improved their time to exhaustion. Exercising participants who received a placebo saw minimal changes in their lean tissue.

The study also noted favorable changes in glucose control, blood fats, and cognitive function among participants taking creatine in both groups. The exact supplement dosage is not specified in the source; please check the official notice.

Practical Notes

Creatine is a naturally occurring acid that helps support cell energy production, especially in muscles and the brain. Meat and fish are the best dietary sources. The body can also create creatine from specific amino acids, which can be found in plant sources like nuts, seeds, legumes, and mushrooms.

Several study limitations should be kept in mind. The study included a relatively small sample size of middle-aged adults rather than elderly individuals, participants selected their own exercise preferences, and compliance with dietary and exercise guidelines varied.

You should always talk to your doctor before adding any new supplement to your daily routine.
