I’m a Geriatrics Doctor—Here Are the 9 Things I Wish More People Understood About Alzheimer’s

Published by AI Pipeline | October 02, 2026

Key Takeaways

  • Care for cognitive health should focus on preserving an individual's quality of life rather than relying solely on statistics.
  • Cognitive changes are not limited to memory loss; they can also involve shifts in mood, personality, language, and behavior.
  • Several medical factors, including thyroid imbalances and vitamin deficiencies, can cause symptoms that mimic dementia.
  • Lifestyle habits play a crucial role in long-term brain wellness alongside genetic risk factors.

What the Source Says

Dr. Pavitri Dwivedi, a geriatrician with the University of Maryland Baltimore Washington Medical Group, emphasizes a person-first approach to Alzheimer's care. While data from an Alzheimer's Association report estimates that millions of older adults live with Alzheimer's disease, Dr. Dwivedi emphasizes that supporting individual functioning and family well-being matters most.

Research suggests that healthy brains undergo natural changes over time. A paper published in Perspectives on Psychological Science notes that older adults may take longer to process new information and recall details with less precision. However, a noticeable shift from a person's individual baseline—such as struggling to cook, manage finances, or navigate familiar roads—warrants clinical attention. Behavioral, mood, or language changes can also signal cognitive shifts.

The source explains that dementia is an overarching term for cognitive decline that interferes with daily life, with Alzheimer's disease being the most frequent cause. Other types include vascular, Lewy body, and frontotemporal dementias, while mild cognitive impairment refers to measurable changes that do not eliminate basic independence. Comprehensive medical evaluations are vital to rule out treatable issues such as medication effects, thyroid problems, stress, hearing loss, or vitamin deficiencies before reaching a diagnosis.

Practical Notes

According to Dr. Dwivedi, family history contributes to cognitive risk, but lifestyle habits strongly influence long-term outcomes. She highlights the "SEEDS" acronym to summarize beneficial habits: sleep, exercise, education or curiosity, diet, and socializing. Additionally, addressing cardiovascular factors like blood pressure, diabetes, and cholesterol, as well as managing hearing loss and avoiding smoking, supports broader wellness.

If you or a loved one notice persistent changes in cognitive abilities, daily functioning, or mood, please talk to your doctor.
