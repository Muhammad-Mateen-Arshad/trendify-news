The Real Story Behind 'The Uprising' and the 1381 Peasants' Revolt, When Thousands Marched on London to Demand a Fairer Society

Imagine the summer of 1381. Across the sunbaked fields of medieval England, a quiet fury reaches its boiling point. Decades after the Black Death devastated the countryside and altered the balance of labor, ordinary working people find themselves pushed to the absolute brink by oppressive poll taxes and the suffocating bonds of serfdom. What begins as localized tax resistance rapidly surges into an unprecedented human wave: thousands of farmers, laborers, and artisans marching inexorably toward London, demanding nothing less than a fairer society.

A Cinematic Awakening

This pivotal, bloody chapter of human history serves as the electrifying backbone for director Paul Greengrass’s new cinematic epic, The Uprising. Starring Andrew Garfield as a fictionalized, unnamed farmer who rises from the mud to spearhead the rebellion, the film strips away polished romanticism to reveal the raw desperation of medieval existence. Garfield’s nameless protagonist serves as a visceral proxy for the millions of anonymous souls who risked everything to challenge an all-powerful monarchy.

The March That Shook the Crown

In June 1381, the impossible happened. The marchers breached London’s gates, torched the strongholds of royal corruptors, stormed the Tower of London, and forced the fourteen-year-old King Richard II into a desperate negotiation. Though the rebellion was ultimately betrayed and met with ruthless royal retaliation, the myth of feudal invincibility was permanently shattered, accelerating the collapse of serfdom across England. Greengrass brings his signature, immersive documentary realism to this clash, grounding an immense historical canvas in the kinetic reality of peasant survival.

Echoes of 1381

  • The Unfair Burden: The revolt was ignited by a third regressive poll tax in four years, demanding equal payment from impoverished serfs and wealthy lords alike.
  • A Radical Vision: Dissident cleric John Ball rallied the marchers with a revolutionary query: "When Adam delved and Eve span, who was then the gentleman?"
  • An Enduring Legacy: Though rebel leaders like Wat Tyler were executed, the Crown never dared to impose a poll tax again for centuries, marking a crucial step toward modern human rights.

Through Garfield's portrayal of a simple farmer transformed by injustice, The Uprising proves that while centuries may pass, the fundamental human fight against tyranny and inequality remains timeless.
