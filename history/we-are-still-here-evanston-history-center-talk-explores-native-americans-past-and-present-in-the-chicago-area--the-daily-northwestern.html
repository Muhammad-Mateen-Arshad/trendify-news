‘We are still here’: Evanston History Center talk explores Native Americans’ past and present in the Chicago area - The Daily Northwestern

Published by AI Pipeline | September 23, 2026

Beneath the modern asphalt and soaring steel of the Chicago metropolitan area lies an ancient, intricate network of trade routes, ancestral gathering places, and sacred landscapes. Long before Evanston’s quiet suburban streets took shape, the southwestern shores of Lake Michigan echoed with the voices of the Potawatomi, Ojibwe, Odawa, Miami, and Ho-Chunk nations. Today, archaeologists and historians are peeling back the layers of concrete to reveal a profound truth that resonates across centuries: this land's First Peoples never vanished.

Echoes Along the Shorelines

For thousands of years, the unique marshy ecosystem of Northern Illinois served as a vital crossroads of Indigenous diplomacy and commerce. Many modern thoroughfares, such as Evanston’s Ridge Avenue and Chicago’s Clark Street, were originally well-worn Indigenous trails designed to navigate the ridge lines of post-glacial terrain. When European settlers arrived, they depended entirely on this ancient infrastructure. Yet, following the 1833 Treaty of Chicago, aggressive government policies forced thousands of Indigenous people from their homelands. Despite these devastating displacements, the physical and spiritual ties to the land proved impossible to sever.

Key Historical Facts

  • Linguistic Roots: The name "Chicago" derives from the Miami-Illinois word Shikaakwa, referencing the pungent wild garlic that grew abundantly along the riverbanks.
  • The Council of Three Fires: A powerful long-standing alliance between the Ojibwe, Odawa, and Potawatomi nations that managed trade, defense, and resource sharing across the Great Lakes watershed.
  • A Resilient Urban Legacy: Mid-20th-century federal relocation programs brought thousands of Native people back to the region, making modern Chicago host to one of the largest and most diverse urban Native American communities in the nation.

Why This Matters

As highlighted by the recent discussion at the Evanston History Center, treating Native American history as a closed chapter conceals a living, evolving reality. Unearthing these buried narratives does more than simply honor the past; it bridges a critical gap between ancient archaeology and modern culture. Ultimately, it reinforces the enduring, powerful declaration of the region's First Nations: "We are still here."
