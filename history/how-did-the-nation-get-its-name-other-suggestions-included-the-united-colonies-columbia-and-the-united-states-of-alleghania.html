How Did the Nation Get Its Name? Other Suggestions Included the United Colonies, Columbia and the United States of Alleghania

Published by AI Pipeline | September 20, 2026

In the stifling late summer of 1776, amid the desperate rumble of revolution, a new power was bleeding into existence. Yet, as armies clashed in the field, the nascent republic remained a phantom—an alliance of thirteen distinct territories united by defiance, but bound without an immortal name.

Forged in the Crucible

On September 9, 1776, the Continental Congress executed a quiet yet seismic stroke of the pen. With a single resolution, the fragile placeholder "United Colonies" was cast into the abyss of history. In its place arose a bold, untested title: The United States of America. It was a moniker crafted not from ancient lineage, but from sheer political audacity, signaling to the grand empires of the Earth that a unified sovereign entity had awakened on the western horizon.

Echoes of Lost Identity

Yet, the adoption of this title was merely the beginning of a centuries-long identity crisis. For two hundred and fifty years, poets, politicians, and philosophers wrestled for the soul of the nation’s name. Many yearned for "Columbia," a lyrical homage that echoed through early anthems and monuments. Others sought to anchor the republic to its wild, untamed interior, championing the "United States of Alleghania"—a majestic nod to the ancient mountain range guarding the frontier. Perhaps most astonishing was a late 19th-century proposal that looked far beyond continental shores, daring to imagine the "United States of the Earth."

Chronicles of the Title

  • The Colonial Shadow: Prior to September 1776, official documents relied on "United Colonies," reflecting a tentative union still tethered to the British realm.
  • The Poet's Alternative: "Columbia" was so deeply revered in the early republic that it nearly supplanted the official name, surviving today in the District of Columbia.
  • The Appalachian Vision: Prominent 19th-century thinkers advocated for "Alleghania" to sever European linguistic ties and honor the continent's native topography.
  • Global Ambition: In 1893, Representative Lucas Miller introduced a constitutional amendment to rename the country the "United States of the Earth," envisioning a nation that would eventually welcome all worldwide states into its union.

As the nation marks a quarter-millennium under its mantle, the titles that fell by the wayside remind us that the name we speak today was never an absolute certainty. It was a choice—a dramatic experiment forged in the fires of revolution.
