Islamabad hints at cautious shift on US sanctions against Iran

Published by AI Pipeline | October 02, 2026

According to Dawn, Pakistan's Foreign Office has indicated a shift in its position regarding recently imposed United States sanctions against Iran. Speaking at a weekly media briefing, Foreign Office spokesperson Sajjad Haider Khan stated that Pakistan remains committed to adhering to all applicable sanctions regimes under both international and domestic laws.

What happened

The revised statement expands on Islamabad's earlier position. According to Dawn, previous statements by former spokesperson Tahir Andrabi emphasized adherence to international law and bilateral trade agreements with Tehran. The updated statement incorporates domestic law and broader applicable sanctions, though the spokesperson did not define which specific restrictions Islamabad considers applicable.

Key facts

  • Pakistan has not officially announced any suspension of trade with Iran or a decision to participate in the US sanctions campaign.
  • Spokesperson Khan reiterated Pakistan's commitment to its strategic partnership with the United States as well as its adherence to a rules-based international system.
  • Pakistan continues to act as a mediator between Washington and Tehran, encouraging both sides to exercise restraint and pursue diplomatic solutions under the Islamabad Memorandum of Understanding.
  • Current State Bank guidelines for trade with Iran require verification that transactions do not involve entities that are subject to international sanctions.

Why it matters

According to Dawn, incorporating domestic law into the Foreign Office's stance offers Pakistani regulators legal flexibility to limit Iran-related financial transactions using national banking, anti-money laundering, and foreign exchange rules. Although unilateral US sanctions do not automatically form binding international law for Pakistan, secondary sanctions create practical risks for foreign businesses. Engagement with sanctioned entities could risk losing access to the US financial system, dollar clearing, correspondent banking, and international networks like SWIFT, which requires member institutions to ensure regulatory compliance.
