Through his high-energy, nationwide grassroots live tour, Rossmann is tapping into a fiery cultural shift: everyday people are done with corporate overreach and are ready to reclaim true ownership of their devices.

The Breaking Point of Consumer Tech

For over a decade, tech giants have systematically eroded the basic concept of personal property. Through anti-repair hardware designs, proprietary screws, software locks, and relentless subscription models, modern gadgets are increasingly treated as temporary rentals rather than permanent purchases. Rossmann’s tour highlights how this aggressive strategy has finally pushed buyers past their limits. The packed crowds at these live events aren't just hardcore tech geeks; they are farmers, independent repair technicians, students, and everyday consumers who have reached a breaking point.

The core grievances fueling this grassroots movement include:

  • Monopolized Repairs: Manufacturers withholding official parts, diagnostic software, and circuit schematics from independent shops.
  • Software Lockouts: Arbitrary remote updates that brick functional hardware or block third-party replacement components.
  • Subscription Creep: Charging monthly fees for physical hardware features that are already built into the device.
  • Planned Obsolescence: Engineering products that are intentionally difficult to open, maintain, or upgrade, forcing premature replacements.

From Online Outrage to Real Legislation

What began as YouTube micro-soldering tutorials and candid commentary has mutated into a legitimate political movement. Rossmann’s live tour acts as both a consumer rights masterclass and a political organizing hub. By bringing these technical hurdles out of internet comment sections and into real-world auditoriums, the tour is mobilizing thousands of voters to demand robust Right to Repair legislation from state and federal lawmakers.

The clear message echoing from every city on Rossmann's tour is that control over our digital tools should not be dictated by corporate boardrooms. As passive frustration transforms into organized advocacy, Big Tech faces a long-overdue reckoning. Consumers are no longer asking for permission to fix what they bought—they are demanding true ownership, and they aren't backing down.
